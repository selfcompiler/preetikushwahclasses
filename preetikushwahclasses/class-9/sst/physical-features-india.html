📍 Six Major Physical Divisions of India

India can be divided into 6 major physiographic divisions, each with distinct landforms, climate, soil, and vegetation.

1. ⛰ The Himalayan Mountains

Young fold mountains running in an arc from west to east; form the northern wall of India

2. 🌿 The Northern Plains

Vast alluvial plains formed by the Indus, Ganga, and Brahmaputra river systems; most fertile region

3. ⛰️ The Peninsular Plateau

Ancient Gondwanaland landmass; triangular plateau of igneous and metamorphic rocks

4. 🏝 The Indian Desert

Sandy Thar Desert west of Aravalli Hills; hot, arid, low rainfall region in Rajasthan

5. ⚓ The Coastal Plains

Narrow strips of land between mountain ranges and the sea; Western and Eastern Coastal Plains

6. 🏖 The Islands

Lakshadweep (coral, Arabian Sea) and Andaman & Nicobar (volcanic/sedimentary, Bay of Bengal)

💡 Memory Trick for 6 Divisions: "Himalaya Needs Peaks, Desert Covers India" → Himalayas, Northern Plains, Peninsular Plateau, Desert, Coastal Plains, Islands.
⛰ The Himalayan Mountains

The Himalayas are the world's highest mountain ranges — young fold mountains formed by the collision of the Indian and Eurasian tectonic plates.

Key Features
  • Spread over an arc of 2,400 km from the Indus gorge (west) to the Brahmaputra gorge (east)
  • Width varies from 400 km in Kashmir to 150 km in Arunachal Pradesh
  • Altitude varies from 5,000 m to 8,000 m+
  • Rivers like Indus, Ganga, and Brahmaputra originate here
  • Snow-fed rivers — keep flowing throughout the year (perennial)
Three Parallel Ranges (West to East)
RangeAlso Known AsAvg. HeightKey Features
Himadri (Innermost)Greater Himalayas~6,000 mNorthernmost; contains highest peaks (Everest, K2, Kanchenjunga); perennial snow
Himachal (Middle)Lesser/Middle Himalayas3,700–4,500 mPir Panjal, Dhaula Dhar, Mahabharat ranges; hill stations (Shimla, Mussoorie, Nainital, Darjeeling)
Shiwalik (Outermost)Outer/Sub-Himalayas900–1,100 mSouthernmost; less consolidated; forms longitudinal valleys called Duns
Important Valleys
  • Between Himadri and Himachal: Kashmir Valley, Kullu Valley, Kangra Valley
  • Between Himachal and Shiwalik: Longitudinal valleys called Duns (e.g., Dehra Dun, Patli Dun, Kotli Dun)
Trans-Himalayas (Tibetan Himalayas)
  • Ranges: Karakoram, Ladakh, Zaskar ranges (north of Himadri)
  • K2 (8,611 m) — second highest peak in the world — lies in the Karakoram
  • This region has a cold desert climate (Ladakh)
The Purvanchal (Eastern Hills)
  • Extension of Himalayas in Northeast India: Patkai, Naga, Manipur, Mizo hills
  • These hills separate India from Myanmar
  • Covered with dense tropical forests
🏭 Important Peaks: Mt. Everest (8,849 m, Nepal–Tibet) — highest in world; Kanchenjunga (8,586 m, India–Nepal border); Nanda Devi (7,816 m) — highest peak entirely within India.
💡 Exam Tip: The three ranges of Himalayas and their characteristics are frequently asked. Remember: Himadri = Highest + Northern + Snow + Perennial rivers; Himachal = Hill stations + Pir Panjal; Shiwalik = Southernmost + Duns.
🌿 The Northern Plains

The Northern Plains are formed by the alluvial deposits of three main river systems: the Indus, Ganga, and Brahmaputra.

Key Features
  • About 2,400 km long and 240–320 km wide
  • Total area: approximately 7 lakh sq km
  • One of the most fertile and densely populated regions in the world
  • Rich in alluvial soil — ideal for agriculture
Four Zones (North to South)
ZoneDescription
BhabarNarrow pebble belt (8–16 km) along Himalayan foothills; rivers disappear underground; not suitable for cultivation
TeraiBelt south of Bhabar; rivers reappear; marshy, wet, dense forest; good wildlife reserves (Kaziranga, Corbett)
BhangarOlder alluvium; elevated upland called doab; contains kankar (calcareous deposits); less fertile
KhadarNewer alluvium in flood plains; renewed every year by floods; most fertile and productive zone
Three Sub-divisions
  • Punjab Plains: Formed by rivers Sutlej, Beas, Ravi, Chenab, Jhelum; called the "Land of Five Rivers"
  • Ganga Plains: Extends from Delhi to Bangladesh; most extensive; drained by Ganga and its tributaries
  • Brahmaputra Plains: In Assam; prone to floods; diverse wildlife (one-horned rhino)
💡 Memory Trick for Zones (N to S): "Big Tigers Bring Kudos" → Bhabar, Terai, Bhangar, Khadar. From mountains southward: first pebbles, then marsh, then old alluvium, then new alluvium.

💡 Exam Tip: The most-asked question is comparing Western Ghats vs Eastern Ghats. Key differences: WG = continuous + steep + Anai Mudi (2695m); EG = discontinuous + lower + Mahendragiri (1501m). Also know: Anai Mudi is in Kerala.
🏝 The Indian Desert (Thar Desert)
  • Located west of the Aravalli Hills in Rajasthan, extending into Pakistan
  • Sandy, undulating terrain with crescent-shaped sand dunes called barchans
  • Annual rainfall is less than 150 mm — very dry and arid
  • Luni river is the only significant river; it drains into the Rann of Kutch
  • Vegetation: cacti, thorny bushes, drought-resistant plants
  • Despite harsh conditions, it is one of the world's most densely populated desert regions
  • Some rivers (Ghaggar) become seasonal or disappear in the sand
🏭 Key Facts: The Thar Desert is one of the world's largest hot deserts. The Rann of Kutch (in Gujarat) is a seasonal salt marsh near the desert. Camels are the traditional means of transport. The Indira Gandhi Canal (Rajasthan Canal) brings water to this arid region.

🧠 Multiple Choice Questions (MCQ)

Click on an option to check your answer. All key topics of Chapter 2 — Physical Features of India are covered.

  • Q1. India is divided into how many major physical divisions?
    • a) 4
    • b) 5
    • c) 6
    • d) 7
    ✅ India has 6 major physical divisions: (1) Himalayan Mountains, (2) Northern Plains, (3) Peninsular Plateau, (4) Indian Desert, (5) Coastal Plains, and (6) Islands. Each has distinct landforms, climate, and vegetation.
  • Q2. The northernmost and innermost range of the Himalayas is called:
    • a) Himadri
    • b) Himachal
    • c) Shiwalik
    • d) Karakoram
    ✅ Himadri (also called Greater Himalayas or Inner Himalayas) is the northernmost, innermost, and highest range with an average height of about 6,000 m. It has the world's highest peaks including Mt. Everest and K2.
  • Q3. The longitudinal valleys between the Himachal and Shiwalik ranges are called:
    • a) Terai
    • b) Bhabar
    • c) Duns
    • d) Khadar
    ✅ The longitudinal valleys between the Himachal (Middle Himalayas) and the Shiwalik (Outer Himalayas) ranges are called Duns. Examples: Dehra Dun, Patli Dun, and Kotli Dun.
  • Q4. Bhangar refers to:
    • a) New alluvial deposits in flood plains
    • b) Old alluvial deposits forming uplands
    • c) A pebble belt along Himalayan foothills
    • d) A marshy belt south of Bhabar
    ✅ Bhangar is the zone of old alluvium that forms the upland areas of the northern plains. It contains kankar (calcareous concretions). It is less fertile than Khadar. Khadar (new alluvium) in the flood plains is the most fertile zone.
  • Q5. The Western Ghats are also known as:
    • a) Sahyadri
    • b) Aravalli
    • c) Vindhya
    • d) Satpura
    ✅ The Western Ghats are also called the Sahyadri. They run parallel to the western coast of India from the Tapti river (Gujarat) in the north to the Nilgiris in the south. The highest peak is Anai Mudi (2,695 m) in Kerala.
  • Q6. Which is the largest saltwater lake in India?
    • a) Dal Lake
    • b) Vembanad Lake
    • c) Chilika Lake
    • d) Pulicat Lake
    ✅ Chilika Lake in Odisha is the largest saltwater lake (and largest coastal lagoon) in India. It is located on the eastern coastal plains (Northern Circar region) along the Bay of Bengal. It is a Ramsar wetland of international importance.
  • Q7. Lakshadweep Islands are of which origin?
    • a) Volcanic
    • b) Coral
    • c) Sedimentary
    • d) Metamorphic
    ✅ Lakshadweep Islands are of coral origin. They are located in the Arabian Sea, consist of 32 islands and islets, cover an area of only 32 sq km, and their capital is Kavaratti.
  • Q8. The only active volcano in India is located on:
    • a) Narcondam Island
    • b) Barren Island
    • c) Pamban Island
    • d) Car Nicobar
    ✅ Barren Island (in the Andaman & Nicobar Islands, Bay of Bengal) is the only active volcano in India. Narcondam Island in the same group has a dormant volcano.
  • Q9. The Deccan Plateau generally slopes towards the:
    • a) West
    • b) North
    • c) East
    • d) South
    ✅ The Deccan Plateau broadly slopes from west to east. This is why all the major rivers of the plateau (Godavari, Krishna, Mahanadi, Kaveri) flow eastward and drain into the Bay of Bengal. The Western Ghats act as the water divide.
  • Q10. The highest peak in the Peninsular India (Western Ghats) is:
    • a) Mahendragiri (1,501 m)
    • b) Doda Betta (2,637 m)
    • c) Anai Mudi (2,695 m)
    • d) Guru Shikhar (1,722 m)
    ✅ Anai Mudi (2,695 m), located in Kerala in the Western Ghats, is the highest peak in Peninsular India. Doda Betta (2,637 m) is the highest peak in the Nilgiris. Mahendragiri (1,501 m) is the highest peak in the Eastern Ghats.

📄 2-Mark Questions & Answers

Short answer questions (2 marks). Write in 2–3 sentences, covering 2 key points.

  • Q1. Name and describe the three parallel ranges of the Himalayas. 2 Marks
    The Himalayas consist of three parallel ranges:
    (a) Himadri (Greater Himalayas): Northernmost and highest (avg. 6,000 m), contains world's highest peaks like Everest and Kanchenjunga, always snow-covered.
    (b) Himachal (Middle Himalayas): 3,700–4,500 m; Pir Panjal and Dhaula Dhar ranges; famous hill stations (Shimla, Mussoorie).
    (c) Shiwalik (Outer Himalayas): Southernmost (900–1,100 m); less consolidated; has Duns between it and Himachal.
  • Q2. Distinguish between Bhangar and Khadar. 2 Marks
    Bhangar: Older alluvial deposits forming upland areas (doab) in the northern plains. Contains kankar (calcareous concretions); less fertile; sits above flood level.
    Khadar: Newer alluvial deposits in flood plains, renewed every year by river floods; most fertile and productive for agriculture; lower elevation.
  • Q3. Distinguish between Western Ghats and Eastern Ghats. 2 Marks
    Western Ghats (Sahyadri): Continuous range parallel to west coast; steep western escarpment, gentle eastern slope; highest peak = Anai Mudi (2,695 m); act as a water divide.
    Eastern Ghats: Discontinuous, dissected by east-flowing rivers (Godavari, Krishna, Mahanadi, Kaveri); lower elevation; highest peak = Mahendragiri (1,501 m).
  • Q4. Distinguish between Lakshadweep and Andaman & Nicobar Islands. 2 Marks
    Lakshadweep Islands: Located in the Arabian Sea; coral origin; 32 islands; area only 32 sq km; capital = Kavaratti.
    Andaman & Nicobar Islands: Located in the Bay of Bengal; volcanic and sedimentary origin; 572 islands; capital = Port Blair; has India's only active volcano on Barren Island.
  • Q5. Compare the Eastern and Western Coastal Plains of India. 2 Marks
    Western Coastal Plains: Narrow (10–50 km) strip between the Western Ghats and Arabian Sea; no major river deltas; natural harbors; Vembanad Lake and backwaters (Kerala).
    Eastern Coastal Plains: Wider (80–130 km) strip between Eastern Ghats and Bay of Bengal; has large river deltas (Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri); Chilika Lake (largest saltwater lake) is located here.
  • Q6. What is the Terai zone? Why is it important? 2 Marks
    The Terai is a zone of marshy, swampy land south of the Bhabar belt in the northern plains. Rivers that disappear in the Bhabar reappear here. It is characterized by dense forests, tall grasses, and abundant wildlife. It is ecologically very important — major wildlife reserves like Corbett National Park (Uttarakhand) and Kaziranga (Assam) are in or near the Terai.
📚 3-Mark Questions & Answers

Medium answer questions (3 marks). Write in 60–80 words with 3–4 clear, organized points.

💡 Scoring Tip: For 3-mark questions, write 3 distinct points, each in a separate sentence. Use bold keywords.
  • Q1. Describe the formation and key characteristics of the Himalayan Mountains. 3 Marks
    (a) Formation: The Himalayas are young fold mountains formed millions of years ago when the Indian tectonic plate collided with the Eurasian plate. They are still rising due to ongoing tectonic activity, making them prone to earthquakes.
    (b) Extent: They run in an arc of 2,400 km from the Indus gorge in the west to the Brahmaputra gorge in the east, with three parallel ranges — Himadri, Himachal, and Shiwalik.
    (c) Importance: They are the source of perennial rivers (Ganga, Indus, Brahmaputra), protect India from cold Central Asian winds, and cause the monsoon by blocking moisture-laden clouds.
  • Q2. Describe the four zones of the Northern Plains from north to south. 3 Marks
    (a) Bhabar: Narrow pebble belt (8–16 km) along Himalayan foothills where rivers disappear underground; not suitable for agriculture.
    (b) Terai: South of Bhabar; rivers reappear as a marshy belt; dense forests and rich wildlife (e.g., Corbett, Kaziranga); thick forests.
    (c) Bhangar: Older alluvium forming uplands; contains kankar; less fertile than Khadar; called doab between two rivers.
    (d) Khadar: Newest alluvium in flood plains; most fertile zone; renewed annually by river floods; ideal for rice, wheat, and sugarcane cultivation.
  • Q3. Describe the Peninsular Plateau. What makes it geologically unique? 3 Marks
    (a) Ancient origin: The Peninsular Plateau is part of the ancient Gondwanaland supercontinent. It is one of the oldest and most geologically stable landmasses on Earth, composed mainly of igneous and metamorphic rocks.
    (b) Sub-divisions: It has two parts — the Central Highlands (north of Narmada, slopes NE, Malwa Plateau) and the Deccan Plateau (south of Narmada, triangular shape, broad eastward slope).
    (c) Ghats: The Western Ghats (Sahyadri) run along the western edge and the Eastern Ghats along the eastern edge. The Deccan Plateau slopes east, causing major rivers (Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri) to flow into the Bay of Bengal.
  • Q4. Explain the importance of the Northern Plains to India. 3 Marks
    (a) Agricultural importance: The Northern Plains contain the most fertile alluvial soils in India, formed by the Indus, Ganga, and Brahmaputra rivers. They support intensive agriculture — rice, wheat, sugarcane — feeding a huge population.
    (b) Population: These plains are the most densely populated region of India. They contain major cities like Delhi, Lucknow, Kolkata, Patna, and Varanasi.
    (c) Resource richness: The Khadar (new alluvium) flood plains are renewed every year, providing fresh nutrients. The Terai zone is a biodiversity hotspot, and the plains have abundant groundwater, making irrigation easy.
  • Q5. Write a short note on the Islands of India. 3 Marks
    India has two major island groups:
    (a) Lakshadweep Islands: Located in the Arabian Sea, these are 32 coral islands with a total area of just 32 sq km. Capital: Kavaratti. They are India's smallest Union Territory.
    (b) Andaman & Nicobar Islands: Located in the Bay of Bengal, these are 572 islands of volcanic and sedimentary origin. Capital: Port Blair. India's only active volcano is on Barren Island. The 10° Channel separates Andaman from Nicobar.
    (c) Importance: These islands are strategic for India's maritime defense, support rich coral reef ecosystems, and are important tourism destinations.
🌟 5-Mark Questions & Answers

Long answer questions (5 marks). Write 120–150 words. Use: Introduction → 4–5 points → Conclusion.

💡 Scoring Tip: Use 5 clear sub-headings for 5-mark answers. Each sub-heading = 1 mark. Always begin with an introduction and end with a conclusion.
Q1. Describe the Himalayan Mountains. Explain their importance for India. 5 Marks

Introduction: The Himalayas are the world's highest and youngest mountain system, forming India's northern wall and playing a crucial role in shaping its geography.

(1) Formation and Extent: The Himalayas are young fold mountains formed by the collision of the Indian plate with the Eurasian plate. They extend in an arc of 2,400 km from the Indus gorge (W) to the Brahmaputra gorge (E), with widths ranging from 400 km (Kashmir) to 150 km (Arunachal).

(2) Three Parallel Ranges: The Himalayas have three parallel ranges — Himadri (northernmost, highest ~6,000 m, perennial snow, highest peaks), Himachal (middle, 3,700–4,500 m, hill stations like Shimla and Mussoorie, Pir Panjal range), and Shiwalik (southernmost, 900–1,100 m, forms Duns).

(3) Perennial Rivers: The Himalayas are the source of major perennial rivers — Ganga, Yamuna, Indus, Brahmaputra. Fed by glaciers and snowmelt, these rivers flow throughout the year, supporting agriculture and civilization in the plains.

(4) Climate Protection: The Himalayas act as a natural barrier against bitterly cold Central Asian winds, keeping India's climate relatively warm. They also act as a barrier for the monsoon — trapping moisture-laden clouds and causing heavy rainfall on the windward side.

(5) Natural and Economic Importance: The Himalayas provide rich biodiversity, timber resources, medicinal plants, and tourist destinations (hill stations, trekking). They are also of great strategic/defense importance — the high mountain ranges are natural barriers against invasion from the north.

Conclusion: The Himalayas are not just mountains — they are the lifeline of India, providing water, moderating climate, supporting biodiversity, and shielding the country from external threats.

Q2. Describe the Northern Plains of India. What makes them so important for India's economy? 5 Marks

Introduction: The Northern Plains, formed by alluvial deposits of the Indus, Ganga, and Brahmaputra rivers, are one of the world's most fertile and densely populated regions.

(1) Formation: The plains were formed over millions of years by alluvial deposits brought down by three river systems: the Indus (Punjab), Ganga (central plains), and Brahmaputra (Assam). They extend about 2,400 km in length and are 240–320 km wide, covering approximately 7 lakh sq km.

(2) Four Zones: From north to south, the plains consist of: Bhabar (pebble belt, rivers vanish), Terai (marshy, wildlife-rich), Bhangar (old alluvium, upland, kankar deposits), and Khadar (new alluvium, flood plains, most fertile). The Khadar zone is renewed each year, making it ideal for intensive farming.

(3) Agricultural Importance: The plains are the most productive agricultural region in India. Major crops grown include wheat, rice, sugarcane, maize, and pulses. The Punjab Plains are called the "granary of India" for their wheat production. The plains support food security for over a billion people.

(4) Population and Cities: The Northern Plains are the most densely populated region in India. Major cities like Delhi, Lucknow, Varanasi, Patna, and Kolkata are located here. The flat terrain and fertile soil have historically attracted human settlement for thousands of years.

(5) Water Resources: The plains have abundant groundwater accessible by wells and tube wells, making irrigation feasible even in areas away from rivers. This has enabled Green Revolution successes, particularly in Punjab and Haryana.

Conclusion: The Northern Plains are India's agricultural heartland — a product of millions of years of river deposition that now supports the country's food production, largest population, and most important cities.

Q3. Describe the Peninsular Plateau. Discuss the differences between the Western and Eastern Ghats. 5 Marks

Introduction: The Peninsular Plateau is an ancient, geologically stable landmass of igneous and metamorphic rocks, remnant of the ancient Gondwanaland supercontinent.

(1) Formation and Features: The plateau is one of the oldest landmasses in the world. It is broadly triangular in shape, lying between the Narmada river in the north and the ocean in the south. It consists of black soil (Regur) in the Deccan, ideal for cotton cultivation.

(2) Two Sub-divisions: The Central Highlands (north of Narmada) slope northward and are drained by Chambal, Betwa, and Sind rivers (Malwa Plateau). The Deccan Plateau (south of Narmada) is broadly triangular, sloping eastward, drained by Godavari, Krishna, Mahanadi, and Kaveri rivers that empty into the Bay of Bengal.

(3) Western Ghats (Sahyadri): Run continuously parallel to the west coast from Tapti river to Nilgiris. They have a steep western face and gentle eastern slope. They act as an important water divide for Deccan rivers. Highest peak: Anai Mudi (2,695 m) in Kerala. They receive heavy rainfall on the windward (western) side.

(4) Eastern Ghats: Run discontinuously parallel to the east coast, dissected by east-flowing rivers (Godavari, Krishna, Mahanadi, Kaveri). They are lower in elevation. Highest peak: Mahendragiri (1,501 m). The junction of the Western and Eastern Ghats forms the Nilgiri Hills (Doda Betta, 2,637 m).

(5) Other Important Ranges: The Aravalli Hills (NW India) are among the world's oldest mountain ranges. The Vindhyan and Satpura ranges separate north India from south India. The Chhota Nagpur Plateau is rich in minerals (coal, iron ore, mica).

Conclusion: The Peninsular Plateau is India's ancient geological core — its diverse topography (ghats, plateaus, ranges) shapes river systems, climate, soil types, and mineral wealth across central and southern India.

📋 Chapter Summary — Quick Revision
  • 6 Divisions: Himalayan Mtns, Northern Plains, Peninsular Plateau, Indian Desert, Coastal Plains, Islands.
  • Himalayas: Young fold mountains; 2,400 km arc; 3 ranges: Himadri (highest, snow), Himachal (hill stations, Pir Panjal), Shiwalik (Duns). Trans-Himalayas: Karakoram, Ladakh, Zaskar.
  • Northern Plains: 2,400 km long, 7 lakh sq km; 4 zones S to N: Bhabar → Terai → Bhangar → Khadar (most fertile); 3 parts: Punjab, Ganga, Brahmaputra plains.
  • Peninsular Plateau: Ancient Gondwanaland; Central Highlands (N of Narmada) + Deccan Plateau (S of Narmada, slopes E); W Ghats = continuous + Anai Mudi (2695m); E Ghats = discontinuous + Mahendragiri (1501m).
  • Thar Desert: West of Aravalli; sandy barchans; <150mm rain; Luni river; extends into Pakistan.
  • Coastal Plains: W (narrow, Konkan-Kannad-Malabar, Vembanad Lake); E (wider, Northern Circar-Coromandel, Chilika Lake — largest saltwater lake).
  • Islands: Lakshadweep = coral + Arabian Sea + 32 islands + Kavaratti; A&N = volcanic + Bay of Bengal + 572 + Port Blair + Barren Island (active volcano); 10° Channel between Andaman & Nicobar.
🔥 Final Exam Tip: Most frequently asked: Himalayan ranges (3 names + features), Bhabar-Terai-Bhangar-Khadar (4 zones), W Ghats vs E Ghats, Lakshadweep vs Andaman & Nicobar, and Chilika Lake + Barren Island. Learn all the peak heights!
