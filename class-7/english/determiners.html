Determiners

Words that introduce nouns and tell us which one, how many, or whose

📑 ✍️ 🌟 📚 ✨
💡 1. What are Determiners?

A determiner is a word placed before a noun to introduce it. Determiners tell us which one, how many, or whose the noun is. They always come at the beginning of a noun phrase.

👉 Key Definition

  • Determiners always come before a noun (or before an adjective + noun).
  • They make the meaning of a noun specific or general.
  • A noun phrase can have only one determiner at a time.
  • Examples: the book, my pen, this bag, three cats, some water.

🧑 Determiner vs Adjective — Important Difference!

  • Adjective: She has beautiful eyes. → "beautiful" describes the eyes.
  • Determiner: She has these eyes. → "these" introduces / identifies the eyes.
  • Adjectives describe; determiners introduce. Both come before nouns, but they serve different jobs.

🆕 7 Types of Determiners — Quick Overview

Every determiner belongs to one of these seven types:

1. Articles 2. Demonstrative 3. Possessive 4. Quantifiers 5. Numerals 6. Distributive 7. Interrogative
📑 2. Articles — A, An, The

Articles are the most common determiners. They tell us whether we are talking about something specific (the) or general (a / an).

ArticleTypeUsed BeforeExamples
aIndefiniteSingular nouns beginning with a consonant sounda dog, a car, a university*
anIndefiniteSingular nouns beginning with a vowel soundan apple, an hour*, an egg
theDefiniteAny noun when both speaker and listener know which onethe sun, the book I gave you
🎉

⚡ Sound Rule — not spelling!

Use "a" or "an" based on the sound of the next word — not its spelling.
"a university" ✔ (starts with yoo sound)  |  "an hour" ✔ (starts with ow sound — h is silent)

📚 When to use "the"

  • When mentioned for the second time: I saw a dog. The dog was barking.
  • When there is only one in the world: the sun, the moon, the sky
  • With superlatives: the best student
  • With names of rivers, seas, mountain ranges: the Ganga, the Himalayas
  • With musical instruments: She plays the sitar.

❌ Zero Article — No Article at All!

  • Before plural nouns used in a general sense: Dogs are loyal.
  • Before uncountable nouns in general sense: Water is essential.
  • Before proper nouns: Rahul is my friend. (not the Rahul)
  • Before languages, games, meals: She speaks Hindi. We play cricket. Breakfast is ready.
👉 3. Demonstrative Determiners

Demonstrative determiners point to specific nouns and tell us whether they are near or far, and whether they are singular or plural.

DeterminerNumberDistanceExample
thisSingularNear (close to speaker)This book is mine.
thatSingularFar (away from speaker)That building is tall.
thesePluralNear (close to speaker)These mangoes are sweet.
thosePluralFar (away from speaker)Those birds are eagles.
👉

This / These = HERE  |  That / Those = THERE

Think of it as pointing: this/these = you can touch it right now. that/those = it's at a distance. Singular needs is; plural needs are.

📝 Demonstrative Determiner vs Demonstrative Pronoun

  • Determiner: This pen is red. → "this" is followed by a noun (pen).
  • Pronoun: This is red. → "this" stands alone; no noun after it.
  • Key rule: If "this/that/these/those" is followed by a noun → it is a determiner. If it stands alone → it is a pronoun.
👤 4. Possessive Determiners

Possessive determiners show ownership or belonging. They answer the question: "Whose?"

PersonDeterminerExample
1st person singularmymy bag, my friend
2nd personyouryour book, your idea
3rd person singular (male)hishis car, his name
3rd person singular (female)herher dress, her smile
3rd person singular (thing/animal)itsits tail, its colour
1st person pluralourour school, our team
3rd person pluraltheirtheir house, their plan
🔑

its vs it's — A Very Common Mistake!

its = possessive determiner → The cat licked its paw.
it's = short for "it is" → It's a lovely day.
If you can replace it with "it is" — use it's. If not — use its.

✅ Possessive Determiner vs Possessive Pronoun

  • Determiner: This is my pen. → "my" is followed by a noun.
  • Pronoun: This pen is mine. → "mine" stands alone (mine/yours/his/hers/ours/theirs).
  • Remember: my/your/his/her/its/our/their → always before a noun → determiners.
📜 5. Quantifiers

Quantifiers tell us about the quantity (how much / how many) of a noun. Some quantifiers are used with countable nouns, some with uncountable nouns, and some with both.

QuantifierUsed withExample
manyCountable nouns onlymany books, many students
few / a fewCountable nouns onlyfew apples, a few friends
muchUncountable nouns onlymuch water, much time
little / a littleUncountable nouns onlylittle milk, a little sugar
someBoth (positive sentences)some rice, some books
anyBoth (questions & negatives)any milk? / no any books
allBothall students, all water
enoughBothenough time, enough chairs
severalCountable nouns onlyseveral reasons, several days
noBothno money, no students
💬

few vs a few  |  little vs a little

few / little (without "a") = negative meaning → almost none, not enough
→ She has few friends. (unfortunately not many)

a few / a little (with "a") = positive meaning → some, not many but enough
→ She has a few friends. (she has some, which is good)

🔑 some vs any

  • Use some in positive / affirmative sentences: There is some milk in the fridge.
  • Use any in negative sentences: There isn't any milk.
  • Use any in questions: Is there any milk?
  • Use some in questions when offering or requesting: Would you like some tea?
🔢 6. Numeral Determiners

Numeral determiners tell us the exact number of something. They are of two types: Cardinal and Ordinal.

TypeAnswersExamples
Cardinal NumeralsHow many? (exact count)one, two, three, ten, hundred…
I have three pencils.
Ordinal NumeralsIn what order / position?first, second, third, last, next…
She came first in the race.

📚 More Examples

  • I read two books this week. (Cardinal — how many)
  • Take the second left after the park. (Ordinal — which order)
  • She is the first girl to finish. (Ordinal)
  • There are twelve months in a year. (Cardinal)
🔁 7. Distributive Determiners

Distributive determiners refer to individual members of a group, treating them one by one or as two alternatives. They are: each, every, either, neither.

DeterminerMeaningNounVerbExample
eachEvery one individually (from 2 or more)SingularSingularEach student has a book.
everyAll members without exception (3 or more)SingularSingularEvery child needs love.
eitherOne or the other (of two)SingularSingularEither road leads to town.
neitherNot one nor the other (of two)SingularSingularNeither answer is correct.
📉

each / every / either / neither → always Singular!

All four distributive determiners take a singular noun and a singular verb.
✔ Each student has  |  ✔ Every player is
✘ Each students have (wrong!)

👉 each vs every — the difference

  • each → focuses on individuals, can be used for 2 or more: Each of the two girls got a prize.
  • every → focuses on the whole group, used for 3 or more: Every student in the class passed.
  • either → one of two choices: You can sit on either side.
  • neither → none of two: Neither team scored a goal.
❓ 8. Interrogative Determiners

Interrogative determiners are used to ask questions about nouns. They are: which, what, whose.

DeterminerUsed to ask aboutExample
whichChoice from a limited, known setWhich book do you want?
whatOpen question — no specific setWhat colour is your bag?
whoseOwnership / possessionWhose pen is this?

📚 More Examples

  • Which route should we take? (from known options)
  • What kind of music do you like? (open, no limit)
  • Whose umbrella is lying in the hallway? (asking about ownership)
🚨 9. Common Mistakes with Determiners

These are the mistakes students make most often — study them carefully!

I have many homeworks to complete.
✓ I have much homework to complete.
"homework" is uncountable → use much, not "many".
She has few sugar in her tea.
✓ She has little sugar in her tea.
"sugar" is uncountable → use little/a little, not "few".
Each students were given a prize.
✓ Each student was given a prize.
"each" takes a singular noun and singular verb.
I don't have some money.
✓ I don't have any money.
Use any in negative sentences, not "some".
The dog wagged it's tail.
✓ The dog wagged its tail.
its (possessive determiner) ≠ it's (it is). No apostrophe in the possessive.
He is a honest man.
✓ He is an honest man.
"honest" begins with a vowel sound (silent h) → use an.
Neither of them are ready.
✓ Neither of them is ready.
"neither" takes a singular verb.
These informations are wrong.
✓ This information is wrong.
"information" is uncountable → no plural. Use this (singular).

📝 Questions & Answers

1 Mark Questions
Q1. What is a determiner? 1 Mark
A determiner is a word placed before a noun to introduce it. It tells us which one, how many, or whose the noun is. Example: the book, my pen, some water.
Q2. Name the two types of articles. 1 Mark
The two types of articles are:
  • Definite article — the
  • Indefinite article — a and an
Q3. When do we use "an" instead of "a"? 1 Mark
We use "an" before a word that begins with a vowel sound (a, e, i, o, u sound). Examples: an apple, an egg, an hour (h is silent), an umbrella.
Q4. Name the four demonstrative determiners. 1 Mark
The four demonstrative determiners are: this, that, these, those.
(this/these = near; that/those = far; this/that = singular; these/those = plural)
Q5. Name any four possessive determiners. 1 Mark
Any four of: my, your, his, her, its, our, their.
These show ownership and always appear before a noun.
Q6. What is the difference between "few" and "a few"? 1 Mark
few (without "a") = negative meaning — almost none, not enough.
Example: She has few friends. (Unfortunately, not many.)
a few (with "a") = positive meaning — some, though not many.
Example: She has a few friends. (She does have some.)
Q7. Name the four distributive determiners. 1 Mark
The four distributive determiners are: each, every, either, neither.
All four take a singular noun and a singular verb.
Q8. Name the three interrogative determiners. 1 Mark
The three interrogative determiners are: which, what, whose.
They are used before nouns to ask questions.
Q9. What is the difference between "much" and "many"? 1 Mark
many — used with countable nouns: many books, many students.
much — used with uncountable nouns: much water, much time.
Q10. What is a zero article? Give one example. 1 Mark
A zero article means no article is used before the noun. It is used with:
  • Uncountable/plural nouns in a general sense: Water is life.
  • Proper nouns: Priya is my sister.
  • Languages, games, meals: I play chess.
2 Mark Questions
Q11. Explain with examples: "some" is used in positive sentences while "any" is used in negative and interrogative sentences. 2 Marks
some is used in affirmative (positive) sentences:
→ There is some juice in the glass.
→ I have some money with me.

any is used in negative sentences:
→ I don't have any money.
any is also used in questions:
→ Is there any milk left?

Exception: When offering something, we use "some" even in a question:
→ Would you like some tea?
Q12. What is the difference between "each" and "every"? Give examples. 2 Marks
each focuses on individual members one by one and can refer to two or more:
→ Each student in the row stood up.
→ Each of the two players got a medal.

every focuses on the group as a whole and is used for three or more:
→ Every child deserves an education.
→ Every shop was closed on Sunday.

Both take a singular noun and singular verb.
Q13. Distinguish between demonstrative determiners and demonstrative pronouns with examples. 2 Marks
Demonstrative Determiner — used before a noun to point to it:
→ This book is very interesting. (this + noun)
→ These mangoes are sweet. (these + noun)

Demonstrative Pronoun — stands alone, replacing the noun:
→ This is very interesting. (no noun after it)
→ These are sweet. (standing alone)

Key rule: If this/that/these/those is followed by a noun → determiner. If it stands alone → pronoun.
Q14. Explain the use of "the" with four clear rules and examples. 2 Marks
  • Second mention: I saw a cat. The cat was black.
  • Unique things (only one in the world): The sun, the moon, the earth.
  • Musical instruments: She plays the violin.
  • Superlatives: He is the best player in the team.
  • Names of rivers, seas, mountain ranges: The Ganga, the Himalayas, the Pacific.
(Any 4 valid points accepted.)
Q15. What are quantifiers? Name five quantifiers and state whether each is used with countable or uncountable nouns. 2 Marks
Quantifiers are determiners that tell us how much or how many of a noun.

manyCountable onlymany trees
muchUncountable onlymuch rain
a fewCountable onlya few apples
a littleUncountable onlya little water
someBothsome books / some milk
Q16. Identify and name the type of determiner used in each sentence: (a) Those shoes are muddy. (b) Every child needs love. (c) Whose bag is on the desk? (d) I need a little rest. 2 Marks
  • (a) Those — Demonstrative determiner (far, plural)
  • (b) Every — Distributive determiner
  • (c) Whose — Interrogative determiner
  • (d) a little — Quantifier (used with uncountable noun "rest")

📚 Quick Revision — All 7 Types of Determiners

  • 📑 Articles — a, an (indefinite) | the (definite) | zero article. Based on sound, not spelling.
  • 👉 Demonstrative — this (near, singular), that (far, singular), these (near, plural), those (far, plural).
  • 👤 Possessive — my, your, his, her, its, our, their. Show ownership. No noun → it becomes a pronoun (mine, yours…).
  • 📜 Quantifiers — many/few/several (countable) | much/little (uncountable) | some/any/all/enough/no (both). some = positive; any = negative/questions.
  • 🔢 Numerals — Cardinal (one, two, three… = how many) | Ordinal (first, second, third… = in what order).
  • 🔁 Distributive — each, every, either, neither. All four → singular noun + singular verb.
  • ❓ Interrogative — which (limited choice), what (open question), whose (ownership).
  • 🚨 Key mistake: "its" (possessive) ≠ "it's" (it is). "few/little" (negative) ≠ "a few/a little" (positive). "much" for uncountable; "many" for countable.
